## Slide 1: Title

Hi everyone, and thanks for watching. Today I'm presenting a recent survey called A Survey on Agent-as-a-Judge, by Runyang You, Hongru Cai and their colleagues at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, with collaborators from Cambridge, Shandong University and Huawei. ## Slide 2: Agenda

Here is the road map. First, the motivation: why evaluation has become a bottleneck, and where a plain language-model judge runs out of road. Second, a quick refresher on LLM-as-a-Judge and its known biases, because the agentic version builds on top of it. Third, the heart of the paper: what an Agent-as-a-Judge is, and the four-part taxonomy the authors use to organize the field. After that I'll turn to the evidence, meaning the benchmarks and the leaderboard, and I'll spend real time on it because it's where the story gets interesting. Then applications and open problems, how the paper relates to other surveys, and my own two cents. I'll close with key takeaways and links, including the GitHub repo and the Medium article that go with this talk.

## Slide 3: Motivation: evaluation is the bottleneck

Modern AI systems are judged constantly: to compare models, to decide what ships, and increasingly to produce the training signal itself, since reward models and judges feed reinforcement learning and data filtering. Humans are the gold standard, but human annotation is slow, expensive, and people disagree with each other. Classic metrics like BLEU and ROUGE are fast and repeatable, but they need a formal target and don't capture open-ended quality. So the field turned to language models as judges. That worked well for open-ended text. But the authors point to a new kind of problem. When the thing being judged is an agent's trajectory, a code patch, or a domain report, the decisive evidence often isn't in the text at all. The judge can read a story about what happened, but it can't see what actually happened. That mismatch is the motivation for everything else in this paper.

## Slide 4: LLM-as-a-Judge in one slide

Here is the baseline. In the fixed-context setup, a judge model is given the thing being judged, the evaluand, plus some context like a rubric or a reference. It returns a score, a preference between two answers, or a written critique. The paper writes this as a simple function of those two inputs, and that is the key point: whatever is in the packet is all the evidence the judge gets. This approach took off because it scales like a metric but reads like a person. The two big LLM-as-a-Judge surveys, by Li and colleagues and by Gu and colleagues, also catalogue its weaknesses. Position bias, where the answer shown first wins more often. Length bias, where longer answers win. And self-enhancement bias, where a model prefers its own style. These are problems with how a judge reads. The agentic idea targets a different problem: what the judge cannot read at all.

## Slide 5: What "Agent-as-a-Judge" means

Here is the central picture. On the left, the passive judge reads a fixed packet once and answers from its own knowledge. On the right, the agentic judge keeps an evaluation state. It can plan what to check, call tools against an environment, consult peer judges, and read or write memory, and only then give a verdict, ideally with an evidence trail attached. The paper gives an operational definition, and it is deliberately strict. At least one real checking step has to happen, such as fetching evidence, running a targeted verification, or reassessing a judgment using another check's findings, and those findings must feed the verdict. Averaging five identical judge calls doesn't count, and neither do personas that score independently. The paper also notes that control can be fixed in advance, planned from the input, or revised on the fly from observations.

## Slide 6: The taxonomy: four mechanisms

The paper's main organizing idea is to classify judges by how their checking process is built, not by which task they judge. It finds four mechanisms that can be mixed freely. Multi-agent collaboration spreads checking and verdict authority across several evaluators. Tool integration gives the judge ways to observe things outside the prompt, like running code or searching the web. Memory decides which findings are kept, how they are read back, and what triggers an update. And planning makes the checking procedure explicit: what to inspect and how to gather proof. On this figure you can see each mechanism broken into sub-types with example systems that the survey names. In the next few slides I'll go through each of them, and then we'll see which ones show up on the leaderboards.

## Slide 7: Mechanism 1: multi-agent collaboration

The first mechanism uses several evaluators. The authors hand-coded 24 papers and found four kinds of roles. Adversarial roles raise objections, perspective roles read the case through different expertise, analytical roles turn raw material into something checkable, and verdict-control roles merge, stop or reroute. A surprising finding is that a role's name doesn't tell you how it's implemented. A critic can be a persona prompt, a scripted step, a rule-based controller, or a tool-using component. Collaboration comes in three modes. Sequential: each stage uses the previous stage's output, which gives traceability but risks lock-in from an early mistake. Reciprocal: judges read each other's reasoning and revise, which can expose hidden assumptions but can also bury dissent. Parallel: independent checks are combined by a resolver, which is reproducible but has no cross-checking.

## Slide 8: Mechanism 2: tool integration

Tools are how a judge touches the world. The authors hand-coded 34 tool-using judges and counted 60 uses of tool interfaces. Code execution leads with 17, web search follows with 13, then local search over stored documents, vision models, and solvers. Selection is the more interesting question: when does the judge decide to use a tool? The paper describes a ladder of increasing autonomy. At the bottom, fixed rules, like sending math to a solver. Then evidence-triggered use, where the judge reasons alone until it notices thin support and then searches. Then observation-guided use, where one tool's output shapes the next call. At the top is active probing, where the judge creates new evidence by running tests or acting in an environment. Tools serve four roles: expanding knowledge, making evidence inspectable, verifying operationally, and judging an interaction trace.

## Slide 9: Mechanism 3: memory

Memory lets a judge carry findings across steps instead of treating each step as a fresh start. The paper frames it as a write, manage and read loop and asks three questions. What is stored? There are three kinds. Process memory is a running scratchpad inside one evaluation, like the queries and evidence so far. Artifact memory keeps case-specific objects such as image crops or analysis reports to reopen later. Experience memory keeps reusable lessons across cases, like failure pools or critique templates. How is it read? Carried forward whole, condensed first, or retrieved selectively. And what triggers an update? Every step, a budget limit, or a notable outcome. The telling observation is at the bottom: most agentic judges keep only a short within-run buffer, and very few build experience that carries over between cases. Memory is the least developed of the four.

## Slide 10: Mechanism 4: planning

Planning makes the checking procedure explicit. The paper splits it in two. What-to-check planning decides the target. DREAM derives report-specific fact checks before running the evaluation. APRES searches over candidate rubric criteria. Evaluation Agent moves across sub-aspects round by round and stops when the evidence is sufficient. How-to-check planning decides the route to the evidence. EvalPlanner writes an evaluation plan first and then follows it. AgentV-RL splits a solution into atomic claims and verifies them along forward or backward paths. TIM-PRM decides, step by step, whether a check needs vision, outside knowledge or logic. VerifiAgent checks completeness and consistency before choosing a tool. The survey's timeline suggests recent attention is moving toward building atomic, case-specific, independently verifiable criteria before any scoring.

## Slide 11: Benchmarks used to validate judges

Now the evidence. The survey says evaluation hasn't kept pace with method development. Most evidence comes from shared judge-quality benchmarks. On the reward-model side, RewardBench, RM-Bench and JudgeBench are pairwise: the judge sees two answers and picks the better one. IFBench covers instruction following, VerifyBench checks answers against a reference, and ProcessBench asks a judge to find the first wrong step in a reasoning trace. On the natural language generation side, datasets like SummEval, TopicalChat-USR and QAGS compare judge scores to human ratings of summaries, dialogue and stories. The catch the authors stress is that these suites measure preference or reward reliability. They don't isolate planning, tool use, evidence acquisition or stopping. Keep that in mind for the next slide.

## Slide 12: Leaderboard: agentic vs non-agentic judges

Here is the comparison the authors assembled. Five agentic judges, in blue, on the same three benchmarks, against three non-agentic reasoning reward models, in orange. The authors kept published scores where they exist and ran released code to fill in the missing cells, because most original papers don't report all three benchmarks. The hatched bars are entries that were not copied from the original paper's own comparison table; the survey doesn't say which of those were reproduced, so I mark them as a group. Look at the blue solid bar, TIR-Judge. It leads on RewardBench and RM-Bench and is essentially tied for first on JudgeBench. Now look at the other blue bars. Most of them sit below the orange ones, especially on JudgeBench. The paper's own summary is that there is no uniform agentic advantage.

## Slide 13: The techniques behind the top scores

Let me explain the techniques that matter on this leaderboard, as the survey describes them. TIR-Judge puts code execution directly into the judge's reasoning loop. This is what the paper calls operational verification: turning a plausible-looking answer into a passed or failed check. It is the one agentic system that leads clearly. Multi-Agent Verification splits checking into aspects, lets separate judges approve or reject each aspect, and sums the approvals without any discussion. It is the purest parallel design, and it posts the best Chat-Hard score on RewardBench, 80.2. The Agentic Reward Model is a hybrid: a conventional reward model plus verification modules for correctness signals, though those modules use other models internally, which complicates any cost comparison. On the non-agentic side, RRM, RM-R1 and Think-RM are reasoning-trained reward models. They have no tools, but they are strong on fixed answer pairs.

## Slide 14: Uneven gains, and what ablations say

To make the unevenness concrete I computed two gaps from the survey's table. On the left, the dark bars compare the best agentic judge to the best non-agentic judge: plus 3.1 on RewardBench, plus 9.8 on RM-Bench, and minus 1.0 on JudgeBench. The light bars compare medians instead, and now only RewardBench is slightly positive; RM-Bench is minus 7.5 and JudgeBench minus 15.9. In other words, one standout system carries the headline. On the right, the heatmap breaks the best-versus-best gap down by subset. Agentic wins big on RM-Bench chat, math and code, but on JudgeBench math a plain reasoning model is ahead by eleven points. The authors' interpretation is careful: these benchmarks hand over two finished answers, so they only partly test evidence-seeking. The survey itself has no component ablation of its own. For ablations I'll point to the original paper in a moment.

## Slide 15: Applications

The paper's application map splits general domains from professional ones. In the general group: math and code, where execution and solver checks catch errors a reader can miss; fact-checking, where a verdict is weak unless it's backed by retrieved support; conversation, where quality depends on goals and state across turns, so judges use simulated users and memory; and multimodal work, where evidence sits in specific image regions or document pages. In the professional group the stakes rise. Medicine is safety-sensitive and needs expertise and uncertainty handling. Law needs the judge to face opposing arguments rather than a single plausible story. Finance has to track report logic, time, and trajectory failures. Education needs consistent rubrics and gradable evidence. The common thread is that the judge matters most where the right answer depends on something outside the text.

## Slide 16: Open challenges

The paper ends with four challenges, and none of them is cosmetic. Efficiency: every extra action adds cost and latency, and the cost compounds when later checks depend on earlier results, which makes online use and reinforcement-learning loops hard. Process reliability: a judge can fail in the middle, by targeting the wrong claim, retrieving weak evidence, misreading a tool output, or letting a group settle on a wrong answer. Memory can then preserve the error. A verdict can look well supported while its evidence trail is broken. Safety and control: tools widen the attack surface to prompt injection and side effects, and when verdicts become reward signals, systematic errors can be amplified by training. Privacy and governance: persistent memory may hold user data, so you need rules for storage, retention and reuse. The future directions follow naturally: adaptive effort, managed memory, autonomous probing and trained judging policies.

## Slide 17: How this survey fits with other work

To place this survey, I read five related papers. The idea itself comes from Zhuge and colleagues in 2024. They introduced Agent-as-a-Judge and a benchmark called DevAI, with fifty-five tasks and 365 requirements. Against a human majority reference, their agentic judge agreed on about ninety percent of requirements for the OpenHands agent, versus about sixty percent for a plain LLM judge. Their ablation, on the right, shows locating the right files gave the final big jump, while an extra retrieve module added nothing in that setup. Then the two broad LLM-as-a-Judge surveys. Li and colleagues organize the field by what, how and where to benchmark. Gu and colleagues focus on reliability and bias. Two 2026 papers add caution. Norman and colleagues audit twenty-one judges and find rankings can move by up to fourteen positions across benchmarks. And a multilingual study finds only thirty-three of 650 papers address multilingual or low-resource judging.

## Slide 18: My two cents

Now my own opinion, clearly separate from the paper. One: the who-judges-the-judge problem doesn't go away, it moves up a level. An agentic judge has plans, tools and memory, and each can fail quietly, so adopting one means evaluating its process, not just its answer. Two: benchmark agreement is a weaker signal than it looks. The Norman paper shows rankings can shuffle by many places between benchmarks, so a three-point edge in a table deserves a shrug. Three: cost. I expected a large dollar multiplier, but the only hard numbers I could verify, from the original DevAI study, show about eleven minutes and twenty-nine dollars for the plain judge, against about 118 minutes and thirty-one dollars for the agentic one. Time rose tenfold while dollars barely moved. That is one setup, so I wouldn't generalize. The curve on the right is my own conceptual sketch, not data. Practically: use a plain judge when the answer is on the page, and go agentic when the verdict depends on something the page can't show.

## Slide 19: Key takeaways

To wrap up. One: a fixed-context judge can't verify what isn't in its packet, and an Agent-as-a-Judge goes and gets the missing evidence, with its findings actually feeding the verdict. Two: the design space breaks into four mechanisms: teams of judges, tools, memory and planning. Three: code execution and search dominate tool use, and memory across cases is the thinnest part. Four: on three shared reward-model benchmarks the gains are uneven. One agentic system leads, but the median agentic judge trails the plain baselines on two of the three benchmarks. Five: those benchmarks hand the judge finished answers, so they only partly measure evidence-seeking, and dedicated agentic benchmarks are still missing. Six: cost, process reliability, safety and governance are open, and the evaluator now needs evaluating too.
